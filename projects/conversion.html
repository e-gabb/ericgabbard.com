Backflip · Real estate lending

Running the funnel on experiments, not opinions

Ten-plus A/B tests on the acquisition funnel moved conversion 34% and loan applications 26% on 10% less marketing spend. The harder one was a three-variant loan application experiment run against two senior stakeholders who wanted opposite answers — where the work was in designing a metric neither of them could reinterpret afterward.

Role
Head of Product (previously Senior Product Manager)
Scope
Cross-functional pod leadership, funnel analysis, experiment design, metric definition, attribution pipeline debugging, methodology documentation

The problem

Everyone could see that people fell out of the funnel. Nobody could say which drop-off actually cost applications. That gap gets filled with conviction — whoever argues best in the room decides what ships, and because nothing is measured against a real baseline, nobody is ever wrong afterward.

The same gap made the biggest open question unresolvable. The loan application flow needed a decision about how much rate information to show, and when. Two senior stakeholders held opposing positions, and each had a plausible story about which funnel shape proved them right — one reading a shorter funnel as friction removed, the other reading a longer one as qualification working. An experiment dropped into that situation with a loosely defined success metric doesn’t settle anything. It just relocates the argument to after the data comes in.

Ten experiments instead of a redesign

I led a cross-functional pod through the funnel systematically: find the drop-off points that actually cost applications, then test enhancements against them one at a time. More than ten A/B tests over the quarter, against a trailing-twelve-month baseline rather than against last month — a lending funnel is seasonal enough that a short baseline will happily tell you whatever you want to hear.

Doing it as ten tests rather than one redesign is slower to feel like progress and much faster to produce knowledge. A redesign that lifts conversion tells you the new one is better. Ten tests tell you which changes did it, which is what the next quarter is built on.

Conversion rose 34% and loan applications 26% — on 10% less marketing spend. The spend line is the one that matters most: the lift came from the funnel converting better, not from buying more traffic to push through it.

The experiment two people wanted to win

For the rate-disclosure question I designed a three-variant split, one third of ad traffic per arm, and spent the design time on the primary metric rather than on the variants.

The metric had to be funnel-shape-neutral: it could not be gamed by an interpretation that favored a longer or a shorter funnel, because that was precisely the axis the two stakeholders disagreed on. Any metric sitting mid-funnel would have handed one of them the win by construction. Defining it before launch, in writing, is what made post-hoc result-gaming impossible rather than merely impolite.

I also calibrated leadership expectations on statistical power before data collection started, so “we don’t have enough data yet” was a known checkpoint agreed in advance rather than a stall tactic invented in week two by whoever was losing.

The variants were the easy part. The metric was the experiment.

The pipeline was lying to us

Mid-flight, the entry counts didn’t reconcile. Investigating turned up three overlapping causes, which is why it hadn’t been caught earlier — each one alone looked like noise:

  • A bot filter tuned too aggressively, sweeping up real traffic
  • Event coverage gaps in the instrumentation
  • A proxy metric standing in for entry that didn’t track it faithfully

The bot filter was the big one: it was discarding roughly 73% of real mobile sessions. I reversed it and corrected counts across all three arms — entries went from 74 / 41 / 45 to 197 / 127 / 125 — then documented the full methodology, including what had been wrong and how the correction was derived, so the result was reproducible by someone who hadn’t lived through it. In an experiment two people were already prepared to dispute, a correction nobody can audit is not a correction.

The result held

With corrected attribution, the rates-hidden variant led submission conversion 4.5% vs 2.0% over 25 days — and it led on every pull across those 25 days, not only in the final cut. Consistency across pulls matters more than the endpoint gap: it’s the difference between a real effect and a snapshot that happened to land favorably.

Because the metric had been fixed in advance, the decision closed. Neither stakeholder had a reading left to argue, which was the entire point of spending the design time where I spent it.

Tradeoffs

Ten experiments means accepting that most of them will be flat. The discipline is running them anyway, and not quietly reinterpreting the losers into wins — which is the same discipline the metric definition was protecting, just applied ten times instead of once.

A funnel-shape-neutral metric also costs you something real: it deliberately refuses to measure the intermediate steps each stakeholder cared about. That’s a genuine loss of diagnostic detail, traded for a result that could actually end the argument. Worth it here only because the argument was the blocker.

And stopping mid-flight to rebuild attribution cost days of a running experiment. The alternative was a faster answer that nobody should have believed.

Outcome

  • +34% conversion and +26% loan applications on 10% less marketing spend — one quarter against a trailing-twelve-month baseline
  • 10+ A/B tests run against measured drop-off points rather than one redesign
  • Funnel-shape-neutral primary metric defined pre-launch; post-hoc result-gaming closed off and the decision settled
  • Winning variant at 4.5% vs 2.0% submission conversion, leading on every pull across 25 days
  • Attribution corrected mid-experiment — ~73% of real mobile sessions recovered, entries 74/41/45 → 197/127/125, methodology documented
